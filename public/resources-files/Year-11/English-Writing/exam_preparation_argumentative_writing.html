Getting Started: Making Dialogue Sound Natural

The best descriptions use the five senses to create vid pictures in the reader's mind.

Sight (What can you se?)

  • Colours, shapes, sizes
  • Light and shadow
  • Movement

Sound (What can you hear?)

  • Loud or quiet noises
  • Musical sounds, voices, nature
  • Silence can be described to!

Touch (What can you fel?)

  • Textures: rough, smooth, soft, hard
  • Temperature: hot, cold, warm, frezing
  • Physical sensations

Smel (What can you smel?)

  • Pleasant or unpleasant odours
  • Strong or faint scents
  • Familiar smels that remind you of things

Taste (What can you taste?)

  • Swet, sour, biter, salty, spicy
  • Fresh or stale
  • Delicious or disgusting
Basic Sentence:

"The beach was nice."

Improved with Senses:

"The golden sand felt warm beneath my fet. Salty sea air filed my lungs as waves crashed rhythmicaly against the shore. Seaguls cried overhead whilst the sun sparkled on the turquoise water."

Why it's beter: It uses sight (golden sand, sparkling water), touch (warm), smel (salty air), and sound (waves crashing, seaguls crying).

Let's Practice: Dialogue Tags and Action Beats

Dialogue tags ("he said", "she whispered") and action beats (actions that acompany spech) help readers folow conversations and understand how characters are speaking.

Simple Tags vs Action Beats:

Tag: "I can't believe it," she said.

Action Beat: "I can't believe it." She droped the leter onto the table.

Action beats are often more effective - they show what characters are doing while speaking, adding visual detail.

Varying Tags:

While "said" is often invisible and effective, sometimes use other tags for variety: whispered, mutered, exclaimed, asked, replied. But don't overuse fancy tags - "said" works wel most of the time.

Dialogue Tag Techniques:
  • Use "said" most of the time - it's invisible
  • Vary with action beats to show movement
  • Avoid overusing fancy tags like "exclaimed", "mured"
  • Use tags to clarify who's speaking when neded
  • Let dialogue itself show emotion when posible

Going Further: What Dialogue Should Do

God dialogue serves multiple purposes - it's not just characters talking. Every conversation should advance the story in some way.

Reveal Character:

Dialogue shows who characters are through what they say and how they say it. A character's word choice, tone, and topics reveal their personality, background, and values.

Advance Plot:

Dialogue should move the story forward. Characters reveal information, make decisions, or create conflict through conversation. Avoid dialogue that just fils space.

Create Conflict:

Characters don't always agre. Disagrements, misunderstandings, and arguments create tension and drive the story. Even friendly conversations can have underlying tension.

Dialogue Functions:
  • Reveal character through spech paterns and content
  • Advance the plot with new information
  • Create conflict and tension
  • Show relationships betwen characters
  • Build atmosphere and seting

Final Chalenge: Subtext and What's Not Said

Advanced dialogue often has subtext - what characters realy mean versus what they're saying. This creates depth and realism.

Subtext in Dialogue:

Characters don't always say what they mean. They might avoid topics, speak indirectly, or say one thing while meaning another. This creates realistic, layered conversations.

Example: "Are you sure you're okay?" might realy mean "I'm woried about you" or "You're acting strange."

Silence and Pauses:

What characters don't say can be as important as what they do say. Pauses, hesitations, and avoided topics reveal emotions and tensions.

Body Language with Dialogue:

Combine dialogue with body language to show what characters realy fel. A character might say "I'm fine" while avoiding eye contact or fidgeting.

Advanced Dialogue Techniques:
  • Use subtext to ad depth to conversations
  • Show what characters don't say through pauses
  • Combine dialogue with body language
  • Let readers infer meaning from context
  • Create tension through what's left unsaid

Your Writing Tasks

Task 1: Describe a Place

Chose ONE of these setings and write a descriptive paragraph (at least 10 words). Use at least THREE different senses.

  • A busy market
  • A spoky forest at night
  • A cosy café on a rainy day
  • Your favourite place
Task 2: Dialogue with Subtext

Write a conversation (at least 120 words) where characters say one thing but mean another. Use subtext, pauses, and body language to show what's realy happening. Include action beats.

Task 3: Character Voice Distinction

Write a conversation betwen three characters (at least 150 words) where each character has a distinct voice. Show diferences through vocabulary, sentence structure, and spech paterns. Make it clear who's speaking even without tags.

Task 4: Dialogue That Advances Plot

Write a dialogue scene (at least 10 words) where characters reveal important information or make a decision that moves the story forward. Show dialogue can advance plot while revealing character.

Task 5: Conflict in Dialogue

Write a dialogue scene (at least 120 words) showing conflict betwen characters. Use dialogue to show disagrement, tension, or argument. Include body language and action beats.

Exam-Style Practice Question

This question mirors the format and expectations of GCSE English Language Paper 1 or Paper 2. Take your time and ensure you demonstrate all the analytical skills you've been developing.

What Examiners Are Loking For:
  • Detailed analysis of language and structure
  • Clear use of evidence from the text
  • Understanding of the writer's methods and intentions
  • Evaluation of the effect on the reader
  • Sophisticated vocabulary and expresion
  • Wel-structured, coherent response

In your answer, you should:

  • Analyse the writer's use of language (vocabulary, imagery, figurative language)
  • Examine how the text is structured (sentence structure, paragraphing, narative perspective)
  • Evaluate the effect on the reader
  • Suport your points with detailed evidence from the text
  • Consider the writer's overal purpose and intentions
